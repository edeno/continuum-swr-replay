\section{Introduction}

% Draft. The literature numbers (Table~\ref{tab:literature}) come from
% docs/design/replay_detection_comparison/literature_detection_parameters.csv.

A hallmark of intelligence is the ability to use past information to predict future consequences. Doing so requires encoding present experience, retrieving past experience and generating future predictions \cite{ComrieImaginationfundamentalfunction2022,HoneySwitchinginternalexternal2017}. A major goal for cognitive systems neuroscience is to understand how neural activity in the brain relates to externally generated present experience and internally generated past experience and future predictions. However, disentangling how neural activity relates to externally and internally generated processes is challenging. First, internal processes are not, by definition, not directly observable. That is, internal processes do not directly correspond to stimuli in the external environment, meaning that single neuron trial-averaged receptive fields are not effective in characterizing them (ref?). Second, internal processes presumably arise as the result of interaction from many neurons, so any method of identifying internal processes needs to be able to handle information from a population of neurons. Third, external and internal processes are dynamic – there are times when engaging in an internal process is more beneficial and other times when processing external stimuli is more beneficial. Consequently, any method of identifying internal processes needs to be able to distinguish between times when an internal process is engaged versus not. Finally, in many studies, the notion of an internal experience is ill-defined. Thus, any method characterizing internal experience needs to have a precise (but not necessarily correct) definition of that process to be properly critiqued.

A model neural system for this dynamic interchange between external and internal processes is the hippocampus. In the rat hippocampus, pyramidal neurons fire in sequences corresponding to positions of the rat in a spatial environment (place cells \cite{OKeefehippocampusspatialmap1971,OKeefehippocampuscognitivemap1978}) – that is, the firing of the neurons corresponds to observable positions from the external environment. However, when the animal is stopped or asleep, neurons fire in patterns resembling spatial trajectories through the environment, but the animal is not physically moving through the spatial environment – it is not directly observable. This phenomenon, known as hippocampal replay, is the result of population level activity – it arises through the firing of many cells in sequence therefore is not amenable to single neuron analysis. It is also dynamic – at times the hippocampal population represents other positions in the environment (non-local times) and at other times the hippocampal population represents the externally observable position of the rat (local times). Because replay sequences can recapitulate previous spatial trajectories through the environment, they are thought to have a role in storing and updating memories \cite{GillespieHippocampalreplayreflects2021}. But what exactly is considered a replay is often defined by a multitude of inconsistent criteria and indirect features such as the local field potential.

Hippocampal replay is typically discovered in a two step procedure. First, a candidate time of interest is identified by finding sharp wave ripple (SWR) times or high multiunit spiking activity (MUA) times when the animal is immobile. Then, a sequence detection procedure is run to determine if the spiking within the candidate time period is a replay sequence or not. Criterion for both steps vary between studies, but most commonly, local field potential ripple band power (mode \PartZeroBandLowMode--\PartZeroBandHighMode{} Hz, range \PartZeroBandLowMin--\PartZeroBandHighMax{} Hz, Table~\ref{tab:literature}) of electrode contacts located in the hippocampal cell layer or multiunit spiking activity are converted to a z-score and a threshold is defined (mode \PartZeroThresholdMode, range \PartZeroThresholdMin--\PartZeroThresholdMax{} z-score, Table~\ref{tab:literature}), whereby candidate events are defined as the start of the crossing of the threshold and ending at the next crossing of the threshold. These events are typically widened to where the events cross 0 on either side of the candidate event. Finally, additional criteria are applied to decide whether to include the event. These include running speed of the animal (mode \PartZeroSpeedMode{} cm/s, range \PartZeroSpeedMin--\PartZeroSpeedMax{} cm/s, Table~\ref{tab:literature}), number of cells spiking during the event (mode \PartZeroCellsMode, range \PartZeroCellsMin--\PartZeroCellsMax{} cells, Table~\ref{tab:literature}), minimum duration (mode \PartZeroShortestMode{} ms, range \PartZeroShortestMin--\PartZeroShortestMax{} ms, Table~\ref{tab:literature}), and maximum duration (mode \PartZeroLongestMode{} ms, \PartZeroLongestMin--\PartZeroLongestMax{} ms, Table~\ref{tab:literature}). Some studies also require simultaneous ripple band power and multiunit spiking activity or the existence of a sharp wave in the radiatum (Buzsáki, aya lab studies). The number of electrodes used to detect the ripple can vary as well—some studies use one electrode while others use all available.

After candidate times of interest are identified, the Bayesian decoding method is most commonly applied ($\sim$\PartZeroBayesFraction\% of studies, Table~\ref{tab:literature}, \cite{ZhangInterpretingNeuronalPopulation1998,BrownStatisticalParadigmNeural1998}). This consists of dividing the time period into discrete bins (mode \PartZeroTimeBinMode{} ms, range \PartZeroTimeBinMin--\PartZeroTimeBinMax{} ms) and computing the Poisson likelihood for each time and discrete position bin in the spatial environment (mode \PartZeroPositionBinMode{} cm, range \PartZeroPositionBinMin--\PartZeroPositionBinMax{} cm) based on the position receptive field rate estimated when the animal is running. Cells that spike in the same time bin are assumed to be conditionally independent on the latent position representation and combined. This gives a probability of position at each time and position bin as estimated from the neural firing in the candidate time of interest. Then a trajectory finding algorithm is applied — typically a linear fit, radon transformation, weighted correlation, or picking the most likely position bin (maximum a posteriori estimate, MAP estimate). “Shuffle” or permutation tests based on breaking relationships between cell firing, etc etc \cite{FosterReplayComesAge2017} are then used to determine if it is a “significant” replay and included in the analysis.

Although these methods have enabled a wide range of discovery about the nature of replay, there remains room for substantial improvement in terms of detection and characterization of replay. Less attention has been given to the detection of candidate events, even though this dictates what is decoded and analyzed. Including more time or less time than the actual event impacts the time bins used to fit the trajectory to the decoded probabilities and could lead to possible rejection of true replay events. Missing events could lead to incorrect conclusions about the role of replay or simply less statistical power because there are fewer events to analyze. As recording technology enables the simultaneous recording of more brain areas, having inaccurate start and end times of replay events could hamper discovery of the relationship between replay and other other brain areas.

A major issue is that candidate event boundaries are discovered indirectly—the methods do not measure whether the replay phenomenon is happening directly in the spiking activity, they instead find signatures of replay (e.g. SWR via the LFP) and then test the spikes for the replay phenomenon. This can be problematic in several ways. First, the features distinguishing the SWR in the ripple band LFP power exist in a continuum and are not easily distinguished by defining a simple threshold \cite{YuDistincthippocampalcorticalmemory2017,Liuconsensusstatementdetection2022}. This has led to studies using a wide range of thresholds \cite{Liuconsensusstatementdetection2022}. Because these thresholds are often defined by z-score, the thresholds are dependent on the mean of the measured data and the time period over which this mean is taken is not consistent \cite{Liuconsensusstatementdetection2022}. Means are also more susceptible to outliers, which means poor recording quality can affect detection. Additionally, a z-score assumes Gaussian distribution which is not appropriate for long tailed distributions bounded by 0 (ref). This is further complicated by a number of other criteria such as speed, number of cells spiking, minimum and maximum duration, number of electrodes, and thresholds for combining ripples which are inconsistent between studies (ref, Table~\ref{tab:literature}). The use of MUA for detection of candidate events also suffers from the same problems of inconsistent thresholds and variable criteria (Table~\ref{tab:literature}). Furthermore, although events detected using MUA are generally correlated with detected SWRs (ref, I think a Buzsáki paper), they do not perfectly correspond ($\sim$\PartZeroMuaOnlyFraction\% studies use MUA and $\sim$\PartZeroSwrOnlyFraction\% use SWR, Table~\ref{tab:literature}), so it is difficult to compare across replay studies because they can use different events. Second, some recent studies have shown some evidence of replay outside of SWRs \cite{KaeferReplayBehavioralSequences2020,CoulterClosedloopmodulationremote2025,WidloskiFlexiblereroutinghippocampal2022,WidloskiReplaysharpwave2025,McHughOfflinehippocampalreactivation2024}, potentially corresponding to dentate spikes \cite{McHughOfflinehippocampalreactivation2024}. However, it is unclear whether these events would have been detected at different thresholds or parameters or using a different source such as MUA. Therefore, the use of indirect methods to discover times of interest may not be adequate to discover all replay events in the data and could potentially lead to differing conclusions across studies.

One attractive option to identify replay is to directly use a decoded position estimate from spiking activity instead of indirect measures from the LFP or MUA. Indeed, several studies that have reported replay absent SWR activity have identified replay by when the decoded position estimate is moving at high speeds \cite{KaeferReplayBehavioralSequences2020,WidloskiFlexiblereroutinghippocampal2022,WidloskiReplaysharpwave2025} or by when it represents specific target locations \cite{CoulterClosedloopmodulationremote2025}. These methods have primarily relied upon finding the most likely spatial bin (the maximum a posteriori estimate, MAP estimate) or posterior center of mass (COM) at each time point using Bayesian decoding. However, there are several disadvantages to this method. The point estimate (MAP or COM estimate) can be noisy given a smaller sample of cells as there can be time bins without spiking. Consequently, these studies have tended to use large time bins (>80 ms) or adaptive time bins (requiring a certain number of spikes per time bin) which compromise time resolution and make assumptions about the population rate. Using the point estimate also does not take into account the uncertainty of the estimate over position, which can lead to highly variable estimates. Finally, none of these methods explicitly take into account local representations of the animal’s position during immobility or non-local immobile representations which are arbitrarily excluded by high speed criterion \cite{YuDistincthippocampalcorticalmemory2017,DenovellisHippocampalreplayexperience2021}, which could lead to misidentified replay time periods.

Therefore, to understand cognitive processes, we need several key features: We want to write down a mathematical definition of what we mean by cognitive representation. We want to be able to separate the cognitive representation from external (behaviorally-driven) events. We want to write down a model that relates the cognitive representation to the observed neural activity. We want the ability for the representation to be dynamic and operate at a moment-by-moment timescale to capture the changing nature of the representation. State space models have proven to be effective at having such features.

In this paper, we develop a new state space model to confidently assign a probability of non-local from the spiking data. We contrast this model with the typical signatures of replay (SWRs and MUA) and investigate the extent to which they occur individually and together. We show that replay can occur even with low-spiking and low sharp wave ripple power. Importantly, we show that in these cases, PFC neurons seem to still respond, demonstrating their importance.
