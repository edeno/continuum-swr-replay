**Reference comparison and novelty assessment — 17 September 2026**

The strongest contribution suggested by the current results is a characterization of brief, focal representations of reward locations outside detected SWRs, together with evidence that replay content in both SWR classes favors arms with uncertain reward beliefs. The mPFC results could establish that these immobile, non-SWR representations participate in a distributed process, but the available population analysis remains preliminary. Each element has precedents; the specific combination and the comparisons across SWR status are the contribution.

This assessment is relative to the eight PDFs in `references/`, not a systematic search establishing worldwide priority. A second reading checked the main texts, figure captions, methods and available supplements against the current figure drafts, claims registry, synthesis and recent audits. **The closest precedent: Widloski and Foster**

The filename says 2024, but the supplied PDF is the Nature Communications article published on 21 November 2025. The [2026 correction](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72252-8) concerns reference entries. In the supplied article, Figure 1 and PDF pages 2–4 establish ripple/burst-less replay; Figures 2–5 address ripple fields. Their detection criteria require duration greater than 100 ms and spatial dispersion greater than 12 cm, as well as spatial coherence and shuffle significance (PDF p. 11). They explicitly exclude stationary replays in the discussion (p. 9).

This directly preempts three broad novelty claims: the discovery of replay without ripples, detection that does not first require a ripple/burst, and the observation that standard triggering misses replay. Their comparison also includes controls across individual tetrodes, low movement/theta, and spike-matched decoding quality, and shows similar replay speeds with and without ripples/bursts. Their claim is not merely that some replay falls just below one consensus threshold. Ripple/burst-less events are already shorter in their data, and Supplementary Figure 3M reports more of them at the Random well before memory-guided navigation to Home. Thus, shorter duration or a behavioral-context association alone would also be insufficient novelty claims.

The present [Figure 2](../paper/figures/replay_story/figure02_replay_classes.png) describes a different part of the representational repertoire. Session-first, animal-balanced median summaries give approximately 56 versus 101 ms duration and 10 versus 42 cm spatial extent for non-SWR versus SWR-coupled events. Median path allocation is 34% versus 79%, respectively. These are descriptive contrasts consistent across the five animals, not proof of distinct generators. The approximately 60% non-SWR event fraction cannot be directly compared with their approximately 20–24% ripple-and-burst-less fraction: the event populations, minimum durations, required spatial movement, burst criterion and ripple criteria differ.

The useful claim is that allowing focal, brief representations reveals a substantial population with a different content profile. Demonstrating how much of that population fails the earlier duration/spatial criteria would make the extension concrete, while accounting for their 80 ms decoding windows and different spatial metric. Stationary remote representations themselves are already included in Gillespie, as discussed below. Call the events outside **detected** SWRs unless additional controls establish the stronger absence-of-ripples claim.

**The well/path distinction has a substantial precedent in Yu et al. (2017)**

Yu et al. identify immobility-associated place cells selective for individual reward wells, including coherent reactivation of a remote well. Approximately 93% of their SWRs contain either movement- or immobility-associated representations rather than both. Well-associated SWRs are shorter and lower amplitude, including after matching cell/spike participation. PFC responses distinguish the two content classes (Figures 3–6; PDF pp. 6–15).

The paper also directly establishes structured immobility activity **outside SWRs**, which the initial version of this comparison underemphasized. IAPs are identified from well-specific firing after excluding SWRs (Figure 1; PDF p. 3), and Figure 6D–E measures hippocampal–PFC coactivity outside SWRs (pp. 13–14). The distinction is between ongoing representation of the animal's current location and reactivation of a remote location: the latter is demonstrated for IAP ensembles during SWRs (Figure 3 and its supplements; p. 6). The paper does not establish a corresponding class of non-SWR remote-well reactivation events.

The N-wave precedent cited by Yu is [Kay et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17144), checked in response to this distinction. It describes current-location coding during immobility outside SWRs, associated with a distinct network pattern, and persistence of location coding into brief desynchronized sleep periods. Yu relates IAP firing properties to N-wave-coupled cells but does not directly identify N-waves in the foraging recordings. Thus neither non-SWR immobility activity nor its spatial organization is a new observation here; showing **remote** content is essential to the extension.

This is stronger overlap than simply saying the paper studied hippocampal–cortical communication. It anticipates the association between focal well content, short events and weak ripples. Your results could extend remote-well reactivation beyond detected SWRs. However, the data here do not identify the same cell populations, and clusterless decoding alone does not establish that these events are generated by Yu's immobility-associated cells or coupled to N-waves. Those correspondences are hypotheses.

A useful synthesis is that representational form may vary systematically with ripple expression: focal locations are common at low ripple expression, whereas extended paths are enriched among detected-SWR events. Neither binary group differences nor a smooth power distribution establishes two mechanisms or one mechanism, respectively.

**What the two other Yu papers imply for the mPFC claim**

Yu et al. (2018) show that PFC cells with generalized task-phase representations preferentially participate in SWR reactivation. Different hippocampal path locations can engage the same PFC cell, while path-versus-well content produces different PFC responses (Figures 5–6; PDF pp. 5–9). Supplementary Figure 14 controls for ripple-power differences, supporting content-specific coordination beyond an amplitude effect. This makes task phase and hippocampal content relevant explanations for cortical modulation. An event-aligned PFC response alone does not show that cortex represents the same location, retrieves a schema, or receives a broadcast from hippocampus.

Yu and Frank (2021) provide a direct precedent for non-SWR **nonlocal** activity coordinated with PFC. They explicitly exclude SWRs and study isolated spikes during locomotion, with strong theta coupling. PFC activity differs from behaviorally matched controls and predicts hippocampal isolated spiking using cross-validated models, including activity 4–8 theta cycles earlier (Figures 5–7; PDF pp. 8–14). Together with Yu 2017's non-SWR coactivity involving immobility-associated cells, this rules out broad novelty claims about cortical engagement without SWRs or during immobility. Your extension concerns cortical modulation around discrete, decoded **nonlocal** events during immobility.

Two qualifications prevent an overly clean separation. Yu's locomotion criterion is >2 cm/s, whereas this project's immobility criterion is <4 cm/s, so the labels overlap between 2 and 4 cm/s (Yu Methods, PDF p. 16). The theta/speed distributions and a stricter stillness subset therefore matter. Also, their isolated spikes and pairwise coactivity support coherent representations of remote locations or different headings, but do not provide an equivalent decoded event-duration, extent or stationary-versus-trajectory classification. Temporal isolation is not a direct measure of representational duration.

The present [mPFC preview](../paper/figures/replay_story/figure04_mpfc_preview.png) supports taking that extension seriously: firing changes occur around both event classes, and the figure includes a matched-control comparison. Its saved snapshot is a partial production result, with uneven animal coverage. Final inference should follow the completed, validated analysis. The current evidence does not establish equal engagement, direction of communication, transfer of spatial information, or consolidation. Such results would bear on the ripple-dependent cortical-broadcast hypothesis discussed by Widloski and Foster, but event-aligned modulation alone would not refute that hypothesis.

**How reward, uncertainty and choice relate to Gillespie and Gupta**

Gillespie et al. report current-arm enrichment at reward ports, suppressed immediate-past-arm replay, increasing goal replay after repeated rewarded visits, persistent previous-goal replay, and enrichment of less recently visited unrewarded arms (Figures 3–8; PDF pp. 7–14). Their trial-level tests do not find a reliable connection between replay content and the next choice across subjects/analyses. These results make current-arm bias, reward-history associations, weak choice relationships and general history dependence poor novelty claims here.

Two methodological details materially increase the overlap. Their continuous decoding state includes stationary remote events; an explicit stationary classifier labels fewer than 30% of remote events stationary, and removing them leaves the conclusions unchanged (Methods, PDF p. 20). Their conclusions also survive MUA-based event detection instead of SWR triggering (Results p. 5; Methods p. 19). The latter establishes a non-ripple-triggered control, but does not itself show that those events occurred outside SWRs. Neither stationary remote content nor avoiding an obligatory ripple trigger is a sufficient novelty claim.

The graded, probabilistic rewards and candidate-arm belief variance are useful extensions. However, a previous-goal effect is not the same quantity as a last-ten-visits reward fraction or a posterior reward-belief mean. The present previous-goal comparison is unidentified/withdrawn, and the length-adjusted SWR-by-value result depends on estimator. Consequently, the project cannot currently claim to reverse Gillespie's previous-goal finding or establish that replay ignores value. A positive value main effect and a negative SWR-by-value interaction are also different claims.

One correction to the existing synthesis is necessary when describing the precedent: Gillespie did use Poisson GLMs of **arm-specific replay counts/rates**, not only normalized content summaries (Figures 3D–E, 4 and 8). The distinctive analysis here is the separation of event occurrence from allocation conditional on an event, with within-stop timing, self-history and SWR-status comparisons. Rate modeling itself is not new.

Gupta et al. already found replay of paths unvisited for more than ten minutes, greater opposite-side replay when that side was infrequently experienced, and constructed shortcut sequences (Figures 4–6; PDF pp. 5–8). Their strict never-experienced shortcut evidence includes 11 events in one rat; all 19 shortcut events should not be described as never experienced. Their sequence search still requires SWR co-occurrence for retained events (supplementary methods). These are direct precedents for selective sampling beyond recent behavior. They are compatible with an uncertainty account but do not measure reward-belief uncertainty. Your positive candidate-arm uncertainty coefficient gives that idea a quantitative target; it does not by itself establish uncertainty as the explanation of their findings or show that replay performs value updating.

**The precise extension beyond the Comrie bandit manuscript**

The supplied v3.2 manuscript restricts its neural analyses to running above 10 cm/s and explicitly excludes stationary replay periods (PDF p. 30). It shows relative-value-related modulation of nearby alternative paths ahead and behind the animal and prediction of stay/switch choices (Figures 2–3). Crucially, distant representations in unoccupied patches do not favor the subsequently chosen or immediately previous patch (Figure S4D, PDF pp. 39–40). The choice result must not be generalized to all nonlocal content.

Maximum distance between actual and represented position increases around patch entry and follows a time course similar to uncertainty about the upcoming/visited port and local value updating (Figures 4–5 and S5F–H; pp. 13–20, 42). This is an association based on aligned time courses, rather than an adjusted test that the uncertainty of a particular remote candidate predicts its representation. The distance metric also differs from the spatial extent traversed within a replay event.

The present question differs in two ways: it concerns immobility, and its uncertainty covariate describes the **candidate arm represented by the event**, not just the current learning situation. In other words, it asks whether more uncertain alternatives receive more representation. The current [Figure 3](../paper/figures/replay_story/figure03_uncertainty_task.png) reports a 4.9-percentage-point increase in allocation from the lowest to highest within-arm uncertainty quartiles for unchosen wells, positive in all five animals. The adjusted session-median uncertainty coefficient is about +0.35, and the split coefficients are positive in both SWR classes.

This is the most interesting computational extension among the current results. The larger total SWR uncertainty contrast is about +0.18 but approaches zero after the event-length adjustment. Thus, the common association is better supported than an uncertainty mechanism unique to one class. The leaf-only model also does not establish uncertainty-related selection of trunk/path content.

The limits matter: uncertainty is inferred from a behavioral model whose forgetting rule links it to time since a visit. The current models include recency controls, but the stronger within-arm value-versus-uncertainty refit remains pending. Incremental model-fit gains are modest, and the content hierarchy lacks held-out validation. This supports uncertainty-associated allocation, not a uniquely identified uncertainty computation or demonstrated information gain. Given Comrie's own difference between nearby alternatives and distant patches, the results do not establish a general running-versus-immobility dissociation in choice prediction. Such a comparison would require matched content definitions, cohorts and analyses.

**Mallory supplies a competing explanation and a timing requirement**

Mallory et al. find early avoidance of the just-traversed path for roughly three seconds, avoidance of recently replayed content over roughly one second, and later retrospective enrichment. MEC inhibition reduces the late retrospective bias, while neuronal adaptation is a model explanation for self-avoidance (Figures 2–4; PDF pp. 1–7). Their results are therefore more specific than a general relationship between replay and time since stopping.

Their main text also reports robustness to requiring SWR co-occurrence and to alternative event detectors; detection without a mandatory SWR is not unique here either. Their linear-track analysis focuses on replay beginning near the animal and moving away, so their use of a local start does not mean the entire event represents the current location. The separate supplementary PDF was inaccessible during this audit; its detailed controls are reported here only to the extent described in the main text.

The present occurrence models address within-stop time and event self-history. That is valuable, but increased event probability after a recent event can coexist with avoidance of that event's **content**. Likewise, a decoded location behind the animal is not automatically retrospective in the sense of recapitulating the actual preceding path. A convincing content comparison should align events to arrival, account for the unequal dwell distributions after reward/omission, and distinguish event frequency from repeated content. The current data do not demonstrate neuronal fatigue or the MEC mechanism.

**The contributions I would prioritize**

1. **A systematic comparison of brief, focal content across ripple expression.** This extends the repertoire excluded by Widloski–Foster and relates it to Yu 2017's well/path organization. Gillespie already includes stationary remote representations, and Yu 2021 establishes sparse non-SWR remote activity; novelty rests on the joint characterization of form, represented reward locations, uncertainty and cortical association. The strongest examples are remote wells. Full-dataset spike-evidence validation, detector-threshold sensitivity, specificity controls and the broader local-kernel check remain important before treating the approximately 60% prevalence as a settled biological quantity.
2. **Allocation to uncertain alternative reward locations across both event classes.** This advances beyond recency descriptions and beyond uncertainty about the currently visited location. Present the association now; reserve an independent uncertainty-selection mechanism for the identifying refit and predictive validation.
3. **Cortical engagement of immobile, nonlocal events outside detected SWRs.** The extension is event-associated modulation around remote content; non-SWR hippocampal–PFC coactivity involving immobility-associated cells is already present in Yu 2017. Finish the current production/inference work; content-specific PFC analyses would make the result stronger than generic modulation.
4. **Different immediate reward associations despite shared content tendencies.** The reconstructed lag-1 rate ratios are approximately 1.09 for non-SWR and 1.41 for SWR-coupled events on the shared well-anchored cohort. The paired immediate-component contrast is positive in 69.4% of 216 sessions, with the same direction in all five animals. Both classes respond, and both favor uncertain arms. These are modeled associations, not raw event-count ratios or causal gates. Timing and self-history dominate model fit, and the reward block does not improve median conditional AIC, so this is a secondary characterization rather than a standalone functional explanation.

I would describe the present contribution as follows:

> During immobility, hippocampal nonlocal representations include brief, focal reward-location events outside detected sharp-wave ripples. These events are more spatially restricted and less strongly associated with immediate reward than SWR-coupled events, while both populations preferentially represent arms with uncertain reward beliefs. Preliminary recordings show accompanying mPFC modulation.

This wording describes the observed organization and names the provisional cortical result. Claims of a new replay mechanism, distinct cognitive functions, causal uncertainty-driven retrieval, or proof that replay does not support planning exceed the current evidence.
